# Signs of AI writing: checklist for manuscript prose

Use: after drafting, run the grep block at the end, then walk the numbered items. The page calls itself "descriptive, not prescriptive": a sign is a prompt to look harder, not proof, and several signs together matter more than one. The page also warns: "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder." So fix the underlying problem (an empty claim, a vague source, a missing number) by rewriting the sentence. Never swap a listed word for a synonym; the page notes that a listed word being overused "does not imply that its synonyms are also overused", so a synonym swap leaves the empty sentence in place.

## 1. Content

1. Undue emphasis on significance, legacy and broader trends. Words to watch: "stands/serves as", "is a testament/reminder", "a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role/moment", "underscores/highlights its importance/significance", "reflects broader", "symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting", "contributing to the", "setting the stage for", "marking/shaping the", "represents/marks a shift", "key turning point", "evolving landscape", "focal point", "indelible mark", "deeply rooted". Variants: placing the subject amid broader "debates" ("generated debate", "prompted broader reflection"), and a hedging preamble that the subject is minor followed by its importance anyway. Fix: state the fact and its number; delete the significance claim.
2. Canned emphasis on notability, attribution and coverage. Words to watch: "independent coverage", "local/regional/national media outlets", "trade publications", "cited/featured/profiled in", "written by a leading expert", "active social media presence", "was identified by". The page adds that tools with web search attach their own superficial analysis to a named source ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence") whether or not the source says it. Paper form: "Smith et al. highlighted the importance of ..." when Smith et al. say no such thing. Fix: cite what the source measured or stated, and check the page.
3. Superficial analyses, usually a present-participle ("-ing") phrase tacked on the end of a sentence. Words to watch: "highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing ...", "ensuring ...", "reflecting/symbolizing ...", "contributing to ...", "cultivating/fostering ...", "encompassing ...", "enhancing ...", "valuable insights", "align/resonate with". Fix: cut the participle clause, or make it a separate sourced claim with a number.
4. Promotional, advertisement-like language. Words to watch: "boasts a", "vibrant", "rich", "profound", "enhancing", "showcasing", "exemplifies", "commitment to", "natural beauty", "nestled", "in the heart of", "groundbreaking", "renowned", "featuring", "diverse array". The page notes newer models are "more subtly positive" and avoid blunt superlatives such as "the best". Fix: neutral description with a measured value.
5. Vague expression of connection or association. Words to watch: "in connection with/to", "connected with/to", "in association with", "associated with", often as "particularly/widely associated". Fix: state the direct relation (fitted to, measured by, taken from, reported in).
6. Vague attributions and overgeneralised opinions (weasel wording). Words to watch: "Industry reports", "Observers have cited", "Experts argue", "Some critics argue", "several sources/publications" when only a few are cited, "such as" before a list that is in fact complete; also "researchers", "described in scholarship", "modern researchers treat". Fix: the number of studies named equals the number cited; use "such as" only when the list is partial.
7. Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects: "Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces challenges ...", then a vaguely positive close ("Despite these challenges ... continues to thrive") or speculation that initiatives "could enhance" the subject; often a "Future Prospects" or "Challenges and Future Directions" section. The page says the sign is "the rigid formula, not simply the mention of challenges". Fix: state each limit as a finding with its number and stop.
8. "X and Y" section headers, above all "Awards and recognition" or "Recognition". Fix: use plain headings that name a method or a finding (see the overlap rules for standard journal headings).

## 2. Language and grammar

9. High density of "AI vocabulary". The page's list: "Additionally" (especially opening a sentence), "align with", "boasts" (meaning has), "bolstered", "crucial", "deep dive", "delve", "emphasizing", "enduring", "enhance", "fostering", "garner", "highlight" (verb), "interplay", "intricate/intricacies", "key" (adjective), "landscape" (abstract noun), "meticulous/meticulously", "pivotal", "robust", "showcase", "tapestry" (abstract noun), "testament", "underscore" (verb), "valuable", "vibrant". By era: GPT-4 (2023 to mid-2024) used most of the list; GPT-4o (mid-2024 to mid-2025) "align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant"; GPT-5 (mid-2025 on) "emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing" plus the notability phrases of item 2. Grok overuses "causal", "empirical", "correlate" and still "underscore". The page: one or two such words "may be coincidental", many of them is "one of the strongest tells". Read literally and in context (a literal underline is not the sign). Fix: plain verb (shows, gives, is, uses, has); keep a technical term only where it is the term of art.
10. Avoidance of basic copulas ("is"/"are"). Words to watch: "serves as/stands as/marks/functions as/operates as/represents [a]", "boasts/features/maintains/offers [a]" where "has" fits, "refers to"; newer output uses longer forms ("began his career as" for "was"). The page cites a measured drop of over 10% in "is" and "are" in academic writing in 2023. Fix: write "is", "are" or "has".
11. Negative parallelisms, three forms: "Not only ... but (also) ..." / "It is not just ..., it's ..."; "It's not ..., it's ..." / "no ..., no ..., just ..."; and the reverse "Y rather than X". They can also run across two sentences joined by "however". Fix: state Y directly.
12. Openings that treat a title as a proper noun: a first sentence that defines the heading or title as if it were a thing ("[Title] refers to ...", "[Title] is the chronological list of ..."). Fix: start with the subject itself.
13. Rule of three: "adjective, adjective, adjective" or "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase", often to make a superficial analysis look comprehensive. Fix: list only the items that exist; two or four is fine.

## 3. Style and formatting

14. Title heading: a heading repeating the document title before all content. Fix: the title appears once, in the title field.
15. Title case in section headings (capitalising all main words). Fix: sentence case.
16. Headings that contain only other headings, with no text of their own. Fix: give the parent a sentence or remove the level.
17. Overuse of boldface, applied mechanically, often to every instance of a phrase in a "key takeaways" style. Fix: no bold in running text.
18. Inline-header vertical lists: list marker, then a bold inline header, a colon, then the text; variants use •, -, – or numbers typed by hand, or drop the colon. Fix: plain bullets or prose; see the overlap rules for labelled items.
19. Overuse of em dashes, used "in a formulaic, pat way" where people write commas, parentheses or colons, and usually with spaces around them. A July 2026 study cited by the page found that among current models only Claude used em dashes more than professional writers. Fix: zero em dashes in prose.
20. Emoji as formatting, in front of headings or bullets. Fix: none.
21. Unusual tables: small, minimally formatted tables that would read better as a sentence; Markdown table syntax inside another markup. Fix: tables only for data a reader will look up.
22. Curly quotation marks and apostrophes, especially mixed with straight ones in the same text. The page's caveats: Word "smart quotes" and Chicago style produce curly quotes legitimately, and Gemini and Claude "typically do not use curly quotes". Fix: follow the journal template and apply one style throughout.
23. Skipped heading levels (starting at level 3 under level 1). Fix: no skipped levels.
24. Overuse of level-1 headings. Fix: one level-1 heading per document.
25. Thematic breaks (----, ---) between sections. Fix: remove.

## 4. Text meant for the chatbot user

26. Collaborative communication left in the text. Words to watch: "I hope this helps", "Of course!", "Certainly!", "You're absolutely right!", "Would you like ...", "is there anything else", "let me know", "more detailed breakdown", "here is a"; tour-guide openers such as "In this section, we will discuss ..." and "In this article, we will explore ..."; notes such as "Delete this section before submission"; text stating that it was written to the venue's guidelines. Fix: delete. "We" names the authors' actions only ("We fit ...").
27. Disclaimers about knowledge cutoff, source availability or usage. Words to watch: "Up to my last training update", "as of my last knowledge update", "While specific details are limited/scarce ...", "not widely available/documented/disclosed", "in the provided/available sources/search results", "based on available information", "[claim] should be treated as ... rather than ...". The page calls the accompanying "likely" fill-in "entirely speculative". Fix: a claim that data are scarce is a bounded search with a count (sources searched, rows found); otherwise delete it.
28. Phrasal templates and placeholder text: "[Your Name]", "[Describe the specific section ...]", "[link to ...]", "(Add your ... here)", url=URL, "2025-XX-XX" dates, INSERT_SOURCE_URL_30, PASTE_..._HERE, SOURCE_PUBLISHER, comments such as "Add if available with citation". (analogue: "[TBD]", "XX", "??" in the manuscript.) Fix: resolve or remove before release.

## 5. Markup

29. Markdown where the target is another format: **bold**, ## headings, ``` fenced code blocks, or Markdown mixed with the target markup. The page: Markdown alone "is not such a strong indicator"; mixed syntax is. Fix: strip on export to Word or LaTeX.
30. Broken markup: garbled template or macro code. (analogue: broken LaTeX, "??" cross-references.) Fix: build the file and read the output.
31. Internal tool artefacts, which the page calls "an unambiguous indicator": ChatGPT contentReference[oaicite:N], oai_citation, "Name+1"-style link residue (e.g. "Wikipedia+1"), turn0search0, turn0image0, citeturn0news0, citeturn1file0, ({"attribution":{"attributableIndex":"X-Y"}}); Gemini [cite: N] and [span_N](start_span)/(end_span); Grok grok-card / grok_card tags and grok_render_citation_card_json; DeepSeek lenticular brackets with daggers (【85†L261-269】); Perplexity [attached_file:1], [web:1] and ppl-ai-file-upload URLs; unclassified :::writing{variant="document" id="NNNNN"}. Fix: delete, then recheck the source behind the claim.
32. Hallucinated categories and templates that do not exist. (analogue: undefined LaTeX macros, journal template commands that do not exist, citation keys missing from the .bib file.) Fix: build and resolve every warning.

## 6. Citations

33. Broken external links: several dead links in new text, especially ones never captured by web archives. Caveats from the page: paywalled links that work for others, links mangled by bots, truncated pasted URLs. Fix: replace or remove.
34. Invalid DOIs and ISBNs: unresolvable DOIs, ISBNs that fail the checksum. Fix: validate each.
35. DOIs that lead to unrelated articles: a real-looking citation whose DOI belongs to a different paper. Fix: resolve every DOI and compare title, authors, journal and year (citation-verifier skill or by hand).
36. Book citations without page numbers or URLs, or with pages that do not support the sentence. Fix: add the page and read it.
37. Incorrect or unconventional reference use: wrong reuse syntax, a real but irrelevant source attached to a claim, the "↩" character around footnotes. Fix: each citation supports the exact sentence it follows.
38. Tracking parameters: utm_source=openai, utm_source=chatgpt.com, utm_source=copilot.com, referrer=grok.com. Fix: strip, and re-verify the source.
39. References declared but never used in the text, or cited but never defined. (analogue: reference list versus in-text citations.) Fix: reconcile both directions.

## 7. Response letters and revision notes (from the page's comment and edit-summary sections)

40. Comment habits: misquoting guidelines or citing ones that do not exist; long replies split into titled sections; claiming the effort to comply was made; asking critics what exactly to improve; dismissing concerns as speculation; openers such as "I understand your concern regarding ...", "I hope this message finds you well", "I am writing to". Fix: answer each reviewer point with what changed, where (section, line, table), and the number.
41. Edit-summary habits: canned assurance of compliance ("ensured that ... adheres to", "refined", "enhanced", "enriched", "streamlined", "improved", "in compliance with", "clarity", "flow"); procedural statements about what was "preserved/retained/avoided/ensured" or "aimed to"; stress on the presence of sources ("added sourced content", "with peer-reviewed sources") instead of the content; "Addressing reviewer feedback". Fix: state the change itself, briefly and specifically.

## 8. Miscellaneous

42. Pronounced shift in writing style within one author's text, and a mismatch in English variety (several models default to American English). Fix: one variety, per the journal; reread the whole draft in one pass for voice drift.
43. Submission statements that argue the text meets the venue's standards. (analogue: a cover letter claiming the manuscript "meets the journal's standards".) Fix: the cover letter states what the paper reports.
44. Differences between models: the page reports that a focus on broader context is more typical of ChatGPT and Grok than of Gemini and Claude, which tend to be more concise. Fix: none on its own; concise text can still carry items 1-41.
45. Bias in content: the page reports a pro-authoritarian bias in some models as of 2026. Mostly irrelevant to a solubility paper; still check for uncritical praise of any source, institution or model.

## 9. Historical indicators (older models; still checked)

46. Didactic disclaimers. Words to watch: "it's important/critical/crucial to note/remember/consider", "worth noting", "may vary", "It is crucial to differentiate". Fix: delete; state each limit once, concretely.
47. Section summaries: "In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall", a section titled "Conclusion" inside a discussion, or a paragraph that ends by restating its own idea. Fix: each section ends on a new number, not a recap (the journal's Conclusions section is allowed; see overlap rules).
48. Prompt refusal: "as an AI language model", "as a large language model", "I cannot offer ..., but I can ...", "I'm sorry". Fix: delete.
49. Abrupt cut-offs: text that stops mid-sentence. Fix: complete or delete.
50. Outdated access dates in citations (an access date well before the draft date). Fix: correct the date or re-access the source.
51. Lexical diversity, also called elegant variation: rotating synonyms to avoid repeating a word (from repetition penalties in older models). The page notes some non-native writers are taught to do this. Fix: repeat the technical term (gas, solubility, deviation) every time.

## 10. Project rule (author, not from the page)

52. Project-internal artefacts: project tags, rung, KI or lane codes, refit or revision stories, file paths. Fix: run the last grep line and delete hits.

## How to read the list (from the page's caveats)

- Detectors and people are unreliable. Detection tools beat chance but have "non-trivial error rates"; a 2025 study found human ability to tell LLM text from human text no better than random chance, while a 2025 preprint found expert annotators caught 90% of AI text and wrongly flagged 3.3% of human text. Rely on this checklist and on source checks, never on a detector score.
- Signs of human writing the page names: simple "is/has" phrases ("there is a", "it has a"); plain words over stiff synonyms ("wrote" not "authored", "used" not "utilized", "tried" not "attempted", "moved" not "relocated"); superlative or definitive statements ("is the only", "was the first"); hedging qualifiers and intensifiers ("very", "perhaps", "tends to"); isolated wordy constructions ("in order to", "the fact that"); and the ability to explain each choice. In this paper, a definitive statement needs its number, and hedges stay under the skill's own rules.
- Ineffective indicators, not to be used alone: perfect grammar; mixed casual and formal registers; "bland" or "robotic" prose; "fancy", academic or formal prose in general (the sign is the specific words, not formality); transition words in isolation ("Additionally", "Consequently", "Notably" are weak on their own); unsourced content; odd or correct markup.

## Where a sign overlaps the skill's allowed style
Style sources: the author's answers and rulings recorded in RULES.md (short plain headings, bullets for long enumerations, colons before lists and equations, one number per sentence, "agrees best" with a table pointer, future-work bullets) and the older rules in RULES.md Part A. Rule for every overlap: the allowed form must carry content the reader needs; the sign is the same form used as padding.
- Bullets and numbered lists: allowed for a real enumeration (coefficients, limits, future-work tasks, items a reader will look up). AI sign: bullets that restate the previous line, a list where one sentence would do, or three items chosen for rhythm (item 13). Test: delete the bullet; if nothing is lost, it was padding.
- Labelled list items (paper-voice-sw-refresh L4, "Label: sentence."): allowed in long-item lists only, label in plain text, never bold, and each item carries a number where one exists. AI sign (item 18): a bold header before every bullet in every list. Test: no `**` in any label; short items carry no label.
- "Recommendation:" line (paper-voice-sw-refresh): one plain-text line closing a per-gas result, naming the model and the score. AI sign: a bold or repeated label line with no number. Test: the line names the gas, the model and a number.
- Short headings: agreed. AI sign: Title Case (item 15), headings that only hold subheadings (item 16), "Challenges and Future Directions", "Future Outlook" or "Recognition" headings (items 7-8). Standard journal headings ("Materials and methods", "Results and discussion") are the venue's convention, not item 8. Test: each heading names a finding or a method, in sentence case.
- Colons before lists and equations: allowed. AI sign: "Key takeaways:" or a bold-label-colon line (item 18). Test: the colon introduces a real list or equation, not a slogan.
- Short declarative sentences and sections ending on a number: allowed. AI sign: a closing recap (item 47). Test: the last sentence of a section carries a new number, not a restatement.
- Conclusions section: the journal requires one, so it is not item 47. AI sign inside it: "In conclusion" openers and paragraphs that restate the abstract. Test: each conclusion carries its number.
- Future-work list in the Conclusions: allowed. AI sign (item 7): "could enhance", "promising avenues", "paves the way", "for the coming decades". Test: each bullet names a gas, data type or method that is missing.
- Stating the model's limits: allowed and required. AI sign (item 7): "Despite these limitations, the model continues to ...". Test: state the limit with a number and stop.
- Table pointers ("agrees best ... (Table 7)"): allowed. AI sign (items 1, 3): significance wording around the table ("remarkably", "demonstrates the robustness", "highlighting"). Test: the sentence says what the table shows and nothing about how impressive it is.
- Tables: data tables are the norm in this field. AI sign (item 21): a two- or three-row table that a sentence would carry. Test: the table holds values a reader will look up.
- Technical terms that collide with the word lists: "-rich" in a composition ("H2-rich gas") is not item 4 (the grep skips hyphenated forms); "associated with" for an uncertainty or a phase is a direct relation, not item 5; "empirical" for an empirical correlation is the term of art, not item 9; "key" stays out unless no plainer word exists; "robust" stays banned under paper-voice ban #13 unless a dated ruling licenses it. Test: the word names a measurable thing, and no plainer word says the same.
- Hedges: the page lists hedging qualifiers as a human sign, but the paper-voice skills ban vague hedges. The skills win: a hedge stays only when it carries a number (an uncertainty, a range, a count).
- Markdown drafts: the draft is written in Markdown, so item 29 applies to the exported Word or LaTeX text, not to the draft file. Test: no `**`, `##` or ``` survives export.
- Em dashes: both skills ban them, so item 19 is already covered. Curly quotes: take the journal's convention and apply it uniformly.
